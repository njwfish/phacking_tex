\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Published empirical results are usually organized around a central
specification, with robustness checks reported as supporting evidence. Readers
rarely see the full set of analyses that could have produced them, or those
that were run and set aside. Reported estimates and the hidden analysis behind
them open room for p-hacking, questionable research practices, researcher
degrees of freedom, HARKing, and the garden of forking paths, discussed in
varied methodological literatures \citep{simmonsFalsePositivePsychologyUndisclosed2011,johnMeasuringPrevalenceQuestionable2012,kerr1998harking,gelmanLoken2013,head2015extent,wicherts2016degrees}.
Field-level diagnostics, such as aggregations across studies, p-value
distributions, and econometric selection methods, document widespread evidence
of selective reporting and p-hacking \citep{franco2014publication,brodeur2016star,brodeur2020methods,vivalt2019specification,jerke2025publication,andrews2019identification,elliott2022detecting}.
Within a single study, however, few theoretical tools are available to
characterize how much a determined analyst can extract from a given dataset by
searching over regression specifications. Pre-registration improves
replicability but constrains the inductive logic of much statistical work.
In practice, researchers routinely deviate from their plans, sometimes
recovering genuine findings the plan would have suppressed, sometimes engaging
in the very specification search pre-registration was meant to prevent --- and
no integrated framework exists to distinguish them. Beyond pre-registration,
available methods give little analytical traction on how variable
pre-processing propagates to downstream estimates, with current contributions
largely confined to covariate selection. Existing methods further lack smooth
adaptivity across low- and high-dimensional settings.

Consider the following statistical model with independent observations 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:model1}
        y^{(i)} = x^{(i)}_0 \beta_0 + {X^{(i)}_{-0}}^\top \beta_{-0} + \varepsilon^{(i)} \qquad i = 1, \dots, n,
\end{equation}
where $y^{(i)}$ is an outcome of interest, $x^{(i)}_0$ is a treatment of interest, $\varepsilon^{(i)}$ is 
an independent noise term, and $X^{(i)}_{-0}$ is a vector of potential control covariates. A researcher having access
to the dataset $\{x^{(i)}_0, {X^{(i)}_{-0}}, y^{(i)}\}_{i = 1}^n$ may present an estimator $\hat \beta_{0, [p]}$ for $\beta_0$ leveraging 
the full configuration as shown in \eqref{eq:model1}, but may also choose to present an estimator $\hat \beta_{0, S}$ only based on a subset $S \subseteq [p]$ of the available covariates. This may either be motivated by proper model selection considerations, leaving out covariates without a significant effect, or by a desire to showcase a configuration with stronger estimated coefficient $|\hat \beta_0|$, which is typically associated to a more scientifically relevant effect (this work also applies to t-statistics for a testing principle, but this aspect is only presenter after). For any fixed $S \subseteq [p]$, statistical model selection theory provides a complete characterization of 
\begin{equation}
        \hat \beta_{0, S} = (X_S^\top X_S)^{-1} X_S^\top y,
\end{equation}
with omitted variable bias when a covariate $j \in [p]$ such that $\beta_j \neq 0$ is not present in $S$, and variance inflation when a covariate such that $\beta_j = 0$ is present in $S$. On the other hand, our work provides a characterization of this quantity \textit{jointly} on \textit{all subselections} $S \subseteq [p]$, which is a much more difficult problem and requires radically different mathematical theorems. Our results are notably adaptive to the amount of allowed swaps (inclusion or exclusion of covariates) $s$ from any starting configuration.

The rest of the paper develops this program. Section~\ref{sec:setup} introduces
the regression/search notation and the geometric primitives.
Section~\ref{sec:sparse-search} proves comparison results for discrete
covariate search below the critical regime and at the critical scale, then
transfers them to studentized statistics under explicit denominator conditions.
Section~\ref{sec:continuous-relaxation} studies unrestricted low-rank projector
search as a benchmark for the largest possible effect of specification freedom
inside the covariate span. Section~\ref{sec:observational-extensions} extends
the same perspective to regression adjustment, inverse probability weighting,
and augmented inverse probability weighting, where the searched nuisance models
create additional model-based degrees of freedom.
