\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Published empirical results are usually organized around a central
specification, with robustness checks reported as supporting evidence. Readers
rarely see the full set of analyses that could have produced them. The hidden analysis behind reported results
opens the door to the possibility of the questionable research practices discussed in
varied methodological literatures
\citep{simmonsFalsePositivePsychologyUndisclosed2011,johnMeasuringPrevalenceQuestionable2012,kerr1998harking,gelmanLoken2013,wicherts2016degrees}.
Field-level diagnostics, such as aggregations across studies, p-value
distributions, and specification-search measures, document evidence of
selective reporting and p-hacking
\citep{franco2014publication,head2015extent,brodeur2016star,brodeur2020methods,vivalt2019specification,jerke2025publication}.
Related econometric work develops identification, correction, and testing
methods for publication bias and p-hacking
\citep{andrews2019identification,elliott2022detecting}.

Within a single study, however, few theoretical tools are available to
characterize how much a determined analyst can extract from a given dataset by
searching over regression specifications. Pre-registration improves
replicability but constrains the inductive logic of much statistical work.
In practice, researchers routinely deviate from their plans, sometimes
recovering genuine findings the plan would have suppressed, sometimes engaging
in the very specification search pre-registration was meant to prevent --- and
no integrated framework exists to distinguish them. Beyond pre-registration,
available methods give little analytical traction on how variable
pre-processing propagates to downstream estimates, with current contributions
largely confined to covariate selection. Existing methods further lack smooth
adaptivity across low- and high-dimensional settings.

with the full configuration corresponding to \eqref{eq:model1}, but may also choose to present an estimator $\hat \beta_{0, S}$ only based on a subset $S \subseteq [p]$ of the available covariates.

On the one hand, for any fixed $S \subseteq [p]$, classical statistical model selection theory provides a complete characterization of 
\begin{equation}
        \hat \beta_{0, S} = \frac{x_0^\top M_S\, y}{x_0^\top M_S\, x_0},
\end{equation}
where $M_S := I - P_S$ residualizes out the chosen controls, with $P_S$ the orthogonal projector onto $\operatorname{span}(X_S)$ and $I$ the $n \times n$ identity. This estimator carries omitted variable bias when a covariate $j \in [p]$ is omitted despite contributing to the outcome ($\beta_j \neq 0$), and variance inflation when a covariate $j \in [p]$ is included despite not contributing to the outcome ($\beta_j = 0$). 

On the other hand, the researcher may decide to strategically showcase one of the $\hat \beta_{0, S}$'s, selection from the complete process $\{\hat \beta_{0, S}, S \subseteq [p] \}$ using the full information from the dataset $\{x^{(i)}_0, {X^{(i)}_{-0}}, y^{(i)}\}_{i = 1}^n$ --- for example, presenting a coefficient with high magnitude, which is typically thought out as a more scientifically relevant effect. 
We call this strategic ``coordinate search'' and formalize it as the following supremum of stochastic process
\begin{equation}
        \sup_{S \subseteq [p], |S| = s} \hat \beta_{0, S},
\end{equation}
characterized in this work under general assumptions on $X_{-0}, x_0, y$, the structure of the true coefficients $\beta_0, \beta_{-0}$, the covariance structure of the controls $C = \text{Cov}(X_{-0})$, treatments $\sigma_0^2 = \text{Var}(x_{0})$, and their interaction $d = \text{Cov}(x_0, X_{-0})$, and $\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = \text{Var}(\varepsilon)$. Our method also works for the process $\{|\hat \beta_{0, S}|, S \subseteq [p]\}$, or for that of the t-statistic when focusing on the question of statistical significance. The latter is also presented in this work but follows a slightly different geometry that is easier to establish once that of the original process has been properly studied.

To summarize, our mathematical derivations hold for independent, not necessarily identically distributed, rows of data, subgaussian entries for $X_{-0}$, a boundedness condition on the effective signal-to-noise ratio, and generic regularity conditions on the rest. The key load-bearing condition is that the empirical covariance matrix needs to satisfy the restricted isometry property, which holds when the population covariance satisfies a corresponding restricted-isometry condition and under a random design assumption with subgaussian random variables.

\paragraph{Experimental and observational settings bounds.} Our mathematical tools enable us to derive upper and lower bounds for all values of $d$. \end{equation}

\paragraph{Plug-in approximation.} While bounds are the most fundamental concept to derive for understanding the scalings of the problem, actual study of hacking in a specific scientific study requires different tools, and this work present a plug-in approximator of the maximum bias under coordinate search. \nic{just kind of an abrupt transition here} Taking inspiration from Gaussian approximations of supremum of stochastic processes, this work proposes a linearly separable approximator of the supremum of $\{\hat \beta_{0, S}, S\subseteq [n]\}$, with vanishing \textit{relative} error in the case $n \gg s \sqrt{\log e p /s}$, which holds for most practical settings with $s$ moderately small. 

In general, a separable approximator does not exist because the population covariance matrix $C$ may encode an intractable combinatorial structure. But under the assumption that local size-$s$ blocks of $C$ are isotropic ($\sup_{|J|\le 2s}\|R_{JJ}-I\|_{\mathrm{op}}=o(1)$) alongside some weaker conditions, the following holds:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:plugin-general}
        \sup_{|S|=s} \hat\beta_{0,S}
        =\beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{s}\{\xi_{1k}\xi_{2k}\}_{(j)}
        +o_{\mathbb P}\!\Big(\tfrac{s\sqrt{\log(ep/s)}}{n}\Big),
        \qquad
        \begin{cases}
           (\xi_{1k})_k &\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N\big(d_k,\,\sigma_0^2-d^\top C^{-1}d\big) \\
           (\xi_{2k})_k &\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N\big((C\beta_{-0})_k,\,\sigma_\varepsilon^2\big)
        \end{cases},
\end{equation}
with $(\xi_{1k})_k$ and $(\xi_{2k})_k$ all independent and where the $\{ \}_{(j)}$ notation denotes the order statistics. This showcases how the search, even in moderately high dimensions, may be expressed as the sum of top $s$ order statistics of random variables. In the idealized scenario $C  = I, d = \beta_{-0} = 0$, this supremum is still more dangerous that the supremum of single Gaussians, since the independent product $\xi_{1}\xi_{2}$ has an excess kurtosis of 7, meaning a small budget $s$ can surprisingly lead to a lot of displacement. 

This is also shown, under extension results of the article and additional assumptions, to be the case for weighting estimators, whereas for doubly robust estimators, the displacement is rather of the form $\xi_{1}\xi_{2} + \xi_{3}\xi_{4}$ with all four independent Gaussians, which has lower kurtosis and is thus less prone to hacking with few covariates. 

More generally, the plug-in can easily be computed in applications without the need to run multiple configurations, which is a useful diagnostic. Further, even when the assumption does not hold, simulations show that the anisotropic case is less prone to hacking (intuitively because there are less degrees of freedom to explore), meaning the plug-in is still a useful tool.

\paragraph{The extended hacking problem.} While this work extracts many important insights from pushing entropic tools as far as possible to derive bounds and stochastic approximators, the obtained results still suffer from two issues. First, they do not apply to the proportional asymptotic regime, where $p$ and $n$ are of comparable size, which is generally thought to be phenomenologically important when working with high-dimensional data. Second, they are basis dependent, that is, they depend on the original basis the data was observed in. More concretely, this means they are not robust to the most trivial form of pre-processing, that is, linear changes of covariates, which may be an important source of bias from researcher's degrees of freedom despite the fact they only end up reporting a small amount of included covariates $s$.

To adress these issues, we introduce a second model of a researcher optimizing the size of the reported coefficient, this time over all orthogonal projectors $P$ ($P = P^\top = P^2$) onto rank-$s$ or lower subspaces of the control span. Compared to the original ``coordinate search'' problem, we call it ``basis search'', and formalize it as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relaxed-search}
        \hat\beta_{0,s}^\star
        =
        \sup_{\substack{\operatorname{Im}(P)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(X_{-0})\\ \operatorname{rank}(P)\le s}} \hat\beta_0^{(P)},
        \qquad
        \hat\beta_0^{(P)} = \frac{x_0^\top (I-P)\, y}{x_0^\top (I-P)\, x_0},
\end{equation}
such that, evidently, $\sup_{|S|=s} \hat\beta_{0,S} \leq \hat\beta_{0,s}^\star$ by inclusion of supports. This generalizes the coordinate search estimator $\hat\beta_{0,S}$, recovered when $P = P_S$ projects onto $\operatorname{span}(X_S)$. 

While this search space is infinitely larger than the coordinate selection space, this new problem admits a much more complete characterization in the proportional asymptotic regime and the maximum bias quantity is still controlled. Under the limit $n, p \rightarrow \infty, p/n \to \kappa \in (0,1)$, the following holds
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relaxed-rmt}
        \hat\beta_{0,s}^\star
        \;\toP\;
        \beta_0 + q +
        \underbrace{\sqrt{\,\frac{1}{(1-\kappa)\big(1-\tfrac{d^\top C^{-1}d}{\sigma_0^2}\big)}-1\,}}_{\text{variance inflation}}\;\;
        \underbrace{\sqrt{\,\frac{\beta_{-0}^\top C\,\beta_{-0}+\kappa\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{4\sigma_0^2}-q^{2}\,}}_{\text{exploitable outcome energy}} \qquad q = \frac{d^\top\beta_{-0}}{2\sigma_0^2}.
\end{equation}
One surprise of this result is how the dependency on $s$ is lost, showcasing the potential of hacking with very few covariates even when only linear changes of variables are allowed, and starkly contrasts with the coordinate selection version of the result. One second surprise is how precisely high dimensionality is paid: for the basis search, only the regular high-dimensional penalty of $(1-\kappa)^{-1}$ in the variance inflation term, and through $\kappa \sigma_{\varepsilon}^2 / \sigma_0^2$ in the exploitable energy term.
