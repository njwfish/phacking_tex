\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Applied papers in the social sciences typically ask readers to interpret a
main specification. A table may add controls column by column, report
alternative samples, or include a long appendix of robustness checks. But the
substantive claim is usually organized around a preferred estimate: the
coefficient, standard error, and $p$-value in the specification the paper treats
as central.

This convention is statistically innocuous when the central specification is
fixed independently of the data. It becomes harder to interpret when the path
to that specification is itself part of the empirical work. Researchers choose
outcomes, samples, transformations, controls, estimators, and standard errors;
many of these choices are defensible, and many are made while learning from the
data. These choices are often called researcher degrees of freedom. When they
are used, deliberately or not, to make the central estimate larger, more stable,
or more statistically significant, we will call the resulting problem
specification search or p-hacking.

Gelman and Loken's garden of forking paths emphasizes that this multiplicity
can arise even when the researcher runs a single data-contingent analysis.
Specification-curve, multiverse, model-uncertainty, and many-analysts
approaches make the same problem visible by replacing the usual
main-specification-plus-robustness format with a more systematic display of
many reasonable analyses
\citep{gelmanLoken2013,steegenIncreasingTransparencyMultiverse2016,simonsohnSpecificationCurveDescriptive2019,silberzahn2018many,youngModelUncertaintyRobustness2017}.
This paper takes the next step for a canonical class of regression
specifications: it asks how large the interpreted coefficient or $t$-statistic
can become when the central specification is chosen from an admissible menu.

The answer is not determined by the number of specifications alone. It depends
on which choices move the identifying variation, which move the outcome, which
change studentization, and which merely relabel equivalent projections of the
same data. We study these degrees of freedom in a regression problem with a
focal regressor or treatment $x_0$, an outcome $y$, and a matrix of candidate
covariates $X=(x_1,\ldots,x_p)$. A researcher may report the largest
coefficient or largest $t$-statistic obtainable after adjusting for a subset
$S$ of at most $s$ controls. The object of interest is the searched process
\[
        \sup_{|S|\le s}\hat\beta_{0,S}
        \qquad\text{or}\qquad
        \sup_{|S|\le s}\hat t_{0,S}.
\]
It represents the statistical price of allowing the central reported
specification to be selected after seeing the data. The central question is how
this price depends on the geometry of $(x_0,X,y)$ and on the class of
specifications the analyst is allowed to use.

The first organizing distinction is between experimental and observational
geometry. In a randomized experiment, the treatment direction is fixed by the
design and is essentially external to the covariate span. Covariates can still
be searched, and such search can strongly inflate a conventional $t$-statistic;
but the inflation is primarily a high-dimensional noise supremum. Conditional
on the covariates and outcome, random assignment makes accidental
treatment-covariate alignments the relevant degrees of freedom. This is the
regime in which a reduced-form Gaussian approximation to the searched
coordinate process is natural.

Observational designs are different even when all confounding is observed. If
$x_0$ has a systematic component in the span of $X$, then choosing controls
changes the residualized treatment $M_Sx_0$. If the outcome also has structure
in the same covariate directions, then the same choice changes both the
numerator $x_0^\top M_Sy$ and the denominators that define the coefficient and
the $t$-statistic. The analyst is no longer only searching over accidental
correlations. The search can exploit deterministic treatment-covariate and
outcome-covariate geometry. Thus observed confounding alone is enough to make
the specification-search problem qualitatively harder; no appeal to unobserved
confounding is needed.

This distinction appears explicitly in the decomposition developed below. For a
support $S$, the population part of the adjusted coefficient contains a sparse
drift term
\[
        b_C(S)
        =
        \frac{
        \beta_{-0,S^c}^{\top}
        \bigl(d_{S^c}-C_{S^cS}C_{SS}^{-1}d_S\bigr)
        }{
        v_{00}-d_S^\top C_{SS}^{-1}d_S
        },
\]
where $d$ records treatment-covariate covariance, $C$ records covariate
covariance, and $\beta_{-0}$ records outcome signal on the controls. In an
experimental benchmark, $d=0$, and this center collapses to the target effect.
In an observational benchmark, the same term is a primitive part of the search
problem. It measures the degree to which a choice of controls can change the
estimand by changing the residual treatment direction.

Studentization introduces a second kind of active degree of freedom. A search
over coefficients is driven by residualized inner products. A search over
$t$-statistics can also exploit changes in residual treatment energy
$x_0^\top M_Sx_0$ and residual outcome energy $y^\top M_Sy$. The manuscript
therefore separates numerator linearization from denominator flatness. When
sparse projections of treatment and outcome onto the searched covariates are
small, the $t$-statistic is well approximated by an additive reduced-form
coordinate process. When these projections are large, the exact finite-sample
$t$-statistic has degrees of freedom that the reduced form deliberately leaves
out. This is precisely why experimental and observational regimes need to be
kept separate.

The second organizing distinction is the admissible specification class. Most
applied robustness checks search over a sparse discrete menu of conventional
adjustment sets. The size of this menu is combinatorial, but its geometry is
constrained by the observed columns of $X$. We analyze this problem below and
at the critical scale $n\asymp s\log(ep/s)$, where the number of searchable
models is large enough to matter but the analyst is still choosing actual
coordinates. We also study a continuous relaxation in which the analyst may
choose arbitrary rank-$\le s$ projectors inside the covariate span. This
relaxation is not a literal model of ordinary robustness checks. Its role is to
show what happens when the admissible degrees of freedom are enlarged from
coordinate selection to unrestricted low-rank data processing.

The results have three main implications. First, the nominal number of
covariates is not the right measure of researcher discretion. What matters is
the interaction between the search budget, the covariance geometry of the
covariates, and the positions of treatment and outcome relative to that
geometry. Second, experiments are special not because specification search is
harmless, but because randomization turns much of the problem into a
calibratable noise supremum. Third, observational specification search is
harder in a precise sense: once treatment is correlated with observed
covariates, the same degrees of freedom can change the residual treatment
direction, the omitted-variable drift, and the studentizing denominator.

The rest of the paper develops this program. Section~\ref{sec:setup} introduces
the regression/search notation and the geometric primitives.
Section~\ref{sec:sparse-search} proves comparison results for discrete
covariate search below the critical regime and at the critical scale, then
transfers them to studentized statistics under explicit denominator conditions.
Section~\ref{sec:continuous-relaxation} studies unrestricted low-rank projector
search as a benchmark for the largest possible effect of specification freedom
inside the covariate span. Section~\ref{sec:observational-extensions} extends
the same perspective to regression adjustment, inverse probability weighting,
and augmented inverse probability weighting, where the searched nuisance models
create additional model-based degrees of freedom.
