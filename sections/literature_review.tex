\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature-review}

\paragraph{Alternative mechanisms on reporting.} The works on research credibility cited in the introduction are heterogeneous,
resting on different and not always comparable assumptions about the scientific
process. A first strand locates the problem in a direct selection pressure:
significant findings are more likely to be published, leaving, for instance, a
discontinuity in the distribution of reported p-values at the conventional
threshold
\citep{rosenthal1979file,franco2014publication,andrews2019identification}. A
second locates it instead in the researcher's own latitude: among many
defensible analyses, a researcher can converge on the model that delivers the
result they wish to present. Seen through this second mechanism, the same
discontinuity reflects not the filtering of fixed results but a change in the
models researchers present, a process adjacent to but not identical with
researcher self-selection, since altering the model also shifts the research
question slightly \citep{brodeur2016star,elliott2022detecting}.

This second mechanism has been described in a series of applied publications,
whose shared method is to run, whenever the data permit, alternative analyses
across the decision space and to see whether a result still stands once less
favourable choices are made. Re-analysing observational studies in a leading
journal, \citet{lenz2021achieving} find that more than thirty percent owe their
headline significance to control variables whose inclusion is never disclosed,
which makes the choice of controls one of the most consequential of these
decisions. In \citet{silberzahn2018many}, twenty-nine teams analysed one dataset
to answer a single question, whether referees give more red cards to
dark-skinned players, each free to choose its own analysis. Their twenty-one
distinct sets of covariates, with different models and exclusion rules, produced
odds ratios from 0.89 to 2.93, significant for twenty teams and not for nine.
Because the treatment and outcome were fixed before any analysis and no result
was filtered for publication, the study isolates the researcher's latitude
cleanly, and the spread is a lower bound on what a researcher seeking a
particular answer could obtain; \citet{breznau2022hidden} reach the same
conclusion with seventy-three teams. 

A further, methodological line builds tools
rather than findings, some for reporting a study's full range of results in
advance, others for detecting manipulation after publication. A multiverse
analysis belongs to the first kind: reported with the study, it enumerates every
defensible specification and gives the estimate each one yields, so the reader
sees the whole distribution rather than a single chosen point
\citep{steegenIncreasingTransparencyMultiverse2016}. A specification curve
presents that distribution graphically, ordering the estimates and marking the
analytic choices behind each, so the contribution of any one covariate can be
read off as in a sensitivity analysis
\citep{simonsohnSpecificationCurveDescriptive2019}. These devices have strong
data requirements and remain tied to one dataset: they show how wide the range
of results happens to be in a given study, not how wide it can be made in
principle. More fundamentally, they document a meaningul form of dispersion, 
while only implicitly positing the researcher's decision space, among the other 
ingradients of the modeling landscape.

\paragraph{Shared implicits and a common model.} In this push for making explicit the hidden assumptions behind these methodological
proposals, we find that explanations insisting on the researcher's
agency can well be reduced down to two assumptions: an adversarial
publication process that rewards the appearance of strong results; and an
asymmetry of information that keeps the reviewer from inspecting the intermediate
steps able to change what is reported. 

In this sense, they cast the situation as 
a \textit{principal--agent problem} (general citation, even maybe definition in parentheses), 
the reviewer playing the principal and the researcher
the agent. This framing has been made formal by \citet{spiess2018optimal} and
\citet{kasy2022optimal}, but is used here only as a qualitative organising
principle. It opens three axes along which theoretical work can vary: the
criterion the researcher optimises, whether a p-value or the coefficient on a
single fixed treatment; the specification set of alternative models open to
them; and the disclosure rule that fixes the degree of asymmetry. Fixing a
single treatment matters: searching over models for one coefficient is
geometrically distinct from searching across many treatments or outcomes, which
reduces to multiple testing and its classical corrections. The present work
fixes the criterion as the treatment coefficient, the same analysis covering its
t-statistic, which proves easier still to inflate, and the specification set as
the choice of controls in a linear regression, taken in both a coordinate and a
continuous form, with treatment and outcome held fixed. The disclosure rule is
the adversarial one: only the model returning the largest statistic reaches the
reviewer, the supremum $\sup_{|S|=s}\hat\beta_{0,S}$ characterised above.

Under choices close to ours, Leamer's extreme-bounds analysis varies the
included controls and reports the range of the resulting coefficient
\citep{leamer1983con}, and \citet{knaeble2024branch} compute its exact largest
and smallest values over all subsets of controls by branch and bound. Incentive
models of publication reach the same control search but summarise it only
through the sign of the movement between specifications, not its magnitude
\citep{mccloskey2022incentive}. None of these gives the size of the range as a
function of the data, which is what this paper provides. That size depends, more
subtly, on how strongly the treatment is correlated with the controls. Under
randomisation it is uncorrelated, every fixed specification targets the same
effect, and only the act of searching can mislead
\citep{lin2013agnostic,aronowRobinsSaarinenSavjeSekhon2021}. As the correlation
grows the controls enter the identifying argument, so changing them moves the
estimate and the assumptions it rests on together
\citep{cinelliForneyPearl2022crash,cinelliHazlett2020sensitivity,oster2019unobservable},
and it is here that this paper locates the harder problem.

Compared to most of this literature, this literature is leveraging recent progress in high-dimensional probability and contributes to a second strand of technical works.
